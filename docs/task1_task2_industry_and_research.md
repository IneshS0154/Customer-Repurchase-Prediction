# Task 1 -- Understanding the Industry Problem

## 1.1 Industry Background

The consultancy operates within **UK online retail and business-to-business (B2B) gift
wholesale**, a sector that sits at the intersection of e-commerce and bulk trade. Our client
data source is the **Online Retail II** dataset (UCI Machine Learning Repository), which
records approximately **1.07 million invoice lines** generated by a UK-based online gift
wholesaler between **December 2009 and December 2011**.

Unlike a typical consumer e-commerce retailer, this business does not sell primarily to
individual end consumers. Its customer base consists largely of **independent gift shops
and small retailers** who purchase decorative items, novelty gifts, and seasonal stock in
bulk for resale. This distinction matters: transaction volumes per order are higher than in
consumer retail (median basket value of approximately **£275**), and purchasing behaviour is
driven by *stock-replenishment* cycles rather than impulse buying.

Geographically, the business is overwhelmingly UK-concentrated: **85.5% of total revenue**
and approximately **91% of customers** are based in the United Kingdom. The remaining
revenue is distributed across roughly 40 other countries, with Ireland (EIRE), the
Netherlands, Germany, France, and Australia representing the largest international markets.

## 1.2 Organizational Context

The client is best characterised as a **small-to-mid-sized online gift wholesaler** operating
on comparatively thin margins relative to the size of individual transactions. Two structural
features of the business are critical to understanding the problem:

- **Seasonality.** Revenue is strongly concentrated around Q4, with **November revenue
  roughly double** that of a typical month, driven by retailers stocking up ahead of the
  Christmas selling season. This pattern repeats consistently across both years of
  available data (November 2010 and November 2011 both show comparable peaks).
- **Operational rhythm.** The business takes **no orders on Saturdays**, reflecting either a
  B2B ordering pattern (retail buyers ordering during their own business hours) or an
  operational choice by the wholesaler. Order volume peaks between 10:00 and 15:00 on
  weekdays.

## 1.3 Description of the Business Problem

The central organizational problem is that **revenue is highly concentrated among a small
number of customers, and the business has no systematic method for identifying which
customers are likely to stop purchasing.**

Quantitatively, from our descriptive analysis of the customer-level data (5,253 identified
customers after data cleaning):

- The **top 1% of customers generate 31.9%** of total identifiable revenue.
- The **top 10% of customers generate 63.9%** of total identifiable revenue.
- The **top 20% of customers generate 77.2%** of total identifiable revenue.

This is a textbook Pareto (80/20) distribution of customer value, and it means the
business's income is **structurally exposed to the loss of a small number of high-value
buyers**. Compounding this exposure, our analysis of a 90-day forward-looking window shows
that **43.3% of customers did not make a repeat purchase**. Non-repurchase is not a rare
edge case for this business -- it affects nearly half of the customer base -- yet there is
currently no mechanism to flag at-risk customers before they are lost, nor any evidence-based
way to decide how much retention spend a given customer relationship justifies.

## 1.4 Importance of Solving the Problem

The economic case for addressing this problem rests on well-established **retention
economics**: acquiring a new B2B wholesale customer is more costly than retaining an
existing one, because acquisition requires marketing spend, onboarding, and relationship-
building with an unproven buyer, whereas an existing customer already has a demonstrated
purchase history and trust relationship. Given the revenue concentration described above,
losing even a small number of top-decile customers would have a **disproportionate impact**
on annual revenue relative to losing an equivalent number of low-value customers.

Additionally, **undifferentiated retention spending is inefficient**. Contacting every
customer with a discount offer wastes margin on customers who would have repurchased
regardless (encouraging discount-seeking behaviour without incremental benefit), while
failing to prioritise the customers whose departure would be most damaging. A
statistically grounded targeting approach directly addresses this inefficiency.

## 1.5 Stakeholders Affected

| Stakeholder | Interest in the Problem |
|---|---|
| **Marketing team** | Needs to know *which* customers to target for retention and *what* offer to extend, within a limited budget. |
| **Finance team** | Needs to protect profit margin and requires a defensible, quantified justification for retention spend. |
| **Operations / inventory management** | Needs reliable demand forecasts to plan stock and staffing ahead of the concentrated Q4 selling period. |
| **Wholesale customers (small retailers)** | Depend on consistent stock availability and fair, non-discriminatory commercial treatment; are indirectly affected by how the business allocates its attention and offers. |

## 1.6 Business Decisions Influenced by the Problem

1. **Which customers receive retention offers**, and at what intensity (e.g. discount
   email vs. reminder-only contact vs. no contact).
2. **How the marketing budget is allocated** across the customer base -- concentrated on
   high-value at-risk customers, or spread broadly.
3. **How Q4 inventory and staffing are planned**, given the predictable seasonal demand
   spike and the operational absence of Saturday trading.

## 1.7 Consultancy Objectives

Our engagement is structured around four objectives, each of which maps to a specific
analytical task in this report:

1. **Describe** purchasing behaviour and quantify revenue concentration (Task 3).
2. **Test statistically** whether meaningful customer segments (e.g. UK vs. international,
   Q4-acquired vs. not) differ in value and loyalty (Task 4).
3. **Build and compare predictive models** for repurchase probability and future customer
   spend (Task 5), evaluated not just on accuracy but on calibration and expected business
   profit.
4. **Recommend a practical, profit-aware retention strategy** for management, validated
   against real-world constraints identified through literature and industry expert
   feedback (Tasks 10--12).

## 2.5 Research Gap

Synthesising across the anchor papers and the broader churn/CLV literature, a consistent
pattern emerges: **many published studies optimise predictive accuracy while neglecting
interpretability, calibrated probabilities, and profit-based decision-making.** A model
that achieves a marginally higher AUC is not necessarily more useful to a business if its
probability estimates cannot be trusted for a cost/benefit decision, or if its coefficients
cannot be explained to non-technical stakeholders such as a marketing team or a Board of
Directors.

This consultancy project is deliberately designed to address all three gaps
simultaneously:

- **Interpretability** -- via odds-ratio reporting on a penalised logistic regression model,
  rather than a black-box classifier.
- **Calibrated probabilities** -- via explicit calibration-curve and Brier-score evaluation,
  not accuracy or AUC alone.
- **Profit-based decision-making** -- via an expected-profit comparison across targeting
  strategies, directly connecting the statistical model to the business's retention-budget
  decision.
